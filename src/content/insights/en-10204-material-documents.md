“Please include material certificates” can mean different things to different people. A useful industrial RFQ names the document required, the product and material specification, and any traceability or inspection arrangements the buyer needs. That clarity belongs before the order, particularly when a document must reflect tests or witnessing that cannot simply be added later.

EN 10204 concerns types of inspection documents. It is not a shorthand for selecting a material grade, proving suitability for every application, or guaranteeing that any available stock carries the records your project requires. Read it with the applicable product specification, order requirements, and your quality procedures.

## Understand the distinction you are requesting

In brief, a **2.1** declaration states conformity with the order without supplying test results. A **3.1** inspection certificate includes specific inspection results and is validated by the manufacturer’s authorised inspection representative independent of the manufacturing department. A **3.2** certificate adds the specified purchaser or official inspection representative alongside the manufacturer’s representative. The exact arrangements need to match the standard and the order.

For the independently witnessed aspects of 3.2, consult an appropriate inspection organisation. [LRQA’s EN 10204 guidance](https://www.lrqa.com/en-us/resources/en10204-type-3-2-factsheet/) describes its role in this area. The manufacturer and inspection parties, rather than a trading company inventing its own document, must substantiate the required record.

## Start from the application’s actual requirement

Ask the engineering or quality owner to identify which document type the application requires. Copy that requirement into the RFQ along with the material grade, standard, revision where relevant, dimensions, quantity, and any supplementary testing. Do not select a type solely because its number appears more comprehensive.

A request can be very specific: the required document may concern a pressure-retaining component’s material rather than every accessory supplied with an assembly. Make that scope visible. Otherwise, a quotation can appear to include “certificates” while leaving the particular component or material record unresolved.

## Keep item identification connected to the records

Establish how the delivered goods will be linked to the accompanying document. Depending on the product and agreed process, identifiers may include a heat, batch, lot, or other traceable reference. The relevant identifiers should be consistent across the offer, document package, delivery records, and markings or packaging.

Where material is divided, cut, repacked, or supplied from stock, ask the source how the relationship to the original record is preserved. Avoid filling an evidence gap with a generic statement about the supplier. The useful question is whether this delivery can be connected to the records required by the purchase order.

## Resolve availability before comparing prices

Ask the supplier to confirm which documents are actually available for the offered stock. If a mandatory record is missing, keep that exception visible during quote comparison. A lower price with a different documentation package is not automatically a comparable offer.

If someone proposes additional verification, replacement stock, or a different inspection route, ask the responsible quality function to assess it before acceptance. Do not assume an existing document can be renamed or upgraded by adding a stamp. Any proposed route needs an appropriate technical and contractual basis.

## Make receipt checks practical

Give the receiving team the agreed document list before goods arrive. They should know what identifier to match, what quantity or material scope the record covers, and who to contact if something does not align. Request legible complete records rather than cropped images or disconnected pages.

Record unresolved differences and preserve the supplied paperwork and packaging. If the documents do not match the agreed order, obtain clarification before treating the discrepancy as closed. The buyer’s established quality process should govern acceptance; the purchasing team’s job is to make the agreed requirement and supplied evidence available to that process.
