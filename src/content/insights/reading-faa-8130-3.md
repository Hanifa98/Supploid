An FAA 8130-3 can be an important part of an aviation supply package, but the presence of a document is only the start of a review. A procurement team needs to connect the paperwork to the offered item, understand what the originator is stating, and identify anything that needs technical clarification before an order is placed.

This guide is a purchasing checklist, not an instruction for issuing a release or determining airworthiness. Your maintenance and quality procedures, the applicable authority’s requirements, and current official guidance control those decisions.

## Start with the purpose of the form

The FAA calls Form 8130-3 an Authorized Release Certificate, Airworthiness Approval Tag. Its use depends on the activity and the originator. The FAA’s current Part 21 completion guidance is Order 8130.21J; that document should not be treated as a universal maintenance-release manual. Confirm which requirements apply to the particular release. See the [FAA form page](https://www.faa.gov/forms/index.cfm/go/document.information/documentid/186171) and [Order 8130.21J](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/Order_8130.21J.pdf).

For the buyer, the practical question is simple: does the offered paperwork support the specific item and condition being proposed? Do not infer that every source provides the same release package, or that every market description maps neatly onto a field on the form.

## Match the identity before reading the price

Review the part number, description, quantity, and serial number against the quotation and the identifying information supplied for the actual item. Pay attention to suffixes and configuration. A similar description does not resolve a different part number, and a photograph of one serial number does not substantiate the paperwork for another.

For a multi-item certificate, establish which line relates to the offered goods. If a shipment is being split from a larger quantity, ask how the supplied records preserve the relationship. Write down unresolved differences and request an explanation from the source through the supplier before proceeding.

Keep the purchasing record organised around the part and serial or batch identity. A folder named only after a supplier can make later receipt checks unnecessarily difficult when several offers are being compared at once.

## Read the release context and remarks together

The status or work entry and the remarks need to be understood together with the relevant release section. Remarks can contain information important to understanding the scope of the document. Ask your technical reviewer to interpret limitations, referenced work, configuration details, and any statements that affect the planned purchase.

Avoid accepting a cropped screenshot if the missing area contains remarks, signatures, dates, or references. Request a legible complete copy for review, and confirm what original or electronic record will accompany the delivery under the agreed process.

Do not resolve unclear statements by editing the source document yourself. A commercial supplier can obtain clarification; the originator is responsible for its own release information.

## Keep release and installation decisions separate

The FAA explicitly explains that Form 8130-3 does not, by itself, establish eligibility for installation on a particular aircraft, engine, or propeller. The receiver or installer must make the relevant determination. That distinction is stated in the [FAA’s 8130-3 questions and answers](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/designees_delegations/resources/8130-3QA.pdf).

A buyer should therefore involve the responsible technical function where installation eligibility, authority acceptance, life status, or configuration is material to the order. Treat a dual-release request as a specific documentation requirement to verify, not an automatic property of a part listed for sale.

## Turn the review into a clear RFQ

Include the exact part number, quantity, acceptable condition, destination, and required release or trace documents in the enquiry. Add any serialised-history, life-limit, non-incident, or configuration requirements that your organisation actually needs. If a document is mandatory, say so before pricing begins.

Ask the supplier to identify available documents in the offer and disclose gaps before you commit.
